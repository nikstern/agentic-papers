# Summary
This paper develops an agent-based model of site-constant foraging in which a forager first explores a landscape, then selects a foraging site, and finally makes repeated trips between that site and a home base. Using bumble bees in patchy crop and wildflower landscapes, it studies how exploration and site-choice mechanisms shape later harvesting outcomes.

# Why It Matters
It is the most directly relevant paper in the set because it explicitly models repeated trips from a central place and separates exploration, site selection, and exploitation in a central-place style foraging process.

# Method / Setup
The model compares two site-generation methods, random sampling and exploration-based looping forays, with four site-selection rules: random choice, distance from nest, local wildflower density, and net energy return. It evaluates resulting harvesting behavior, travel time, fitness-related outcomes, and crop pollination consequences in simulated agricultural landscapes.

# Key Claims
- Site-selection strategy has a strong effect on crop pollination services and the fraction of crop resources collected.
- Site-generation strategy has a strong effect on trip time and the share of time spent harvesting versus traveling.
- Patterns in downstream foraging outcomes can help infer how real organisms identify foraging sites.

# Limitations
- The model is calibrated around bumble bee foraging in agricultural landscapes, so its conclusions are not automatically general across taxa or environments.
- The mechanism space is deliberately limited to a small set of site-generation and site-selection rules rather than a full behavioral repertoire.
